At most large tech companies, algorithm and coding problems are the biggest part of the loop. They are problem solving questions: the interviewer wants to watch you handle something you have not seen before. You will usually get through exactly one of them in forty-five minutes.

There is no scoresheet. Nobody awards points for using a hash map. Your interviewer forms a judgement across five areas, analysis, coding, computer science fundamentals, experience, and communication, and writes it up. A number may get attached at the end, but it summarises the judgement rather than computing it. And the weighting shifts with the round: a system design interview leans on analysis and experience and barely looks at syntax.

The judgement is also relative. Your interviewer compares you against everyone they have ever asked that question, which has a useful consequence: a hard question is not bad luck, because it was hard for everyone.

One more thing worth understanding. Companies accept that they reject good people. What they guard against is hiring someone who is not good. A false negative costs them one candidate. A false positive costs them years. That asymmetry explains most of the format.
