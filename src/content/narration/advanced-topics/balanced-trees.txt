Feed a plain binary search tree sorted data and it degenerates into a linked list, with every operation dropping to linear time. Self-balancing trees prevent that by reshaping themselves as you insert and delete, and they are the reason a sorted map can promise log n.

An AVL tree stores each node's balance factor, the left height minus the right height, and insists it stay between minus one and plus one. An insert can push a node to plus or minus two, which gets repaired on the way back up the recursion. There are four shapes. Left-left and right-right take a single rotation. Left-right and right-left take two: one to convert them into an easy case, then the easy fix. Crucially, a rotation only re-parents three links and never disturbs the in-order sequence, which is why it is always a legal move.

Red-black trees balance less strictly and rebalance more cheaply. No red node may have a red child, and every path from a node to its leaves holds the same number of black nodes. Put those two together and the longest path can never exceed twice the shortest, which is all that log n needs. That is why most standard libraries choose them.
