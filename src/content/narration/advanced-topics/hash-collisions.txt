Every hash table collides eventually. The key space is larger than the array, so the pigeonhole principle guarantees it. What separates implementations is what happens next.

Separate chaining puts a linked list, or occasionally a balanced tree, in each slot. It degrades gracefully, and it only gets bad with a terrible hash function or adversarial keys.

Open addressing keeps everything in the array. On a collision you probe forward: the next slot for linear probing, quadratic steps for quadratic probing, or a step size from a second hash for double hashing. It is fast and space-efficient, but the table can never hold more entries than it has slots.

Linear probing also suffers from clustering. In a hundred-slot table where slots twenty through twenty-nine are full, the next insert lands on slot thirty if it hashes anywhere in that range: about a ten percent chance rather than one percent. Full runs attract more entries, which makes them longer. Quadratic probing and double hashing exist to break that feedback loop.

The load factor governs everything. Real tables resize and rehash at a threshold, which is amortised constant time.
