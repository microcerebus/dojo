The last family of questions asks you to build one random process out of another, and every one of them has the same trap: a procedure that looks even and is not. You cannot see it by staring. You have to count outcomes.

Building a fair seven-sided die from a fair five-sided one is the standard example. No fixed number of calls can work, because each result would need probability one seventh, and seven never divides a power of five. So you use a loop. Combine two calls into a range of twenty five equally likely values, throw away the four that hang over the edge of a multiple of seven, and take the remaining twenty one modulo seven. Discarding uniformly chosen outcomes leaves the survivors uniform. That is the entire justification.

Shuffling is the other example. Fisher and Yates walk the array and, at each position, swap with a random index drawn from the unfinished part including the current position. That gives exactly n factorial outcomes. The obvious version, swapping with any index in the whole array, gives n to the n paths, and n factorial does not divide that, so some orderings are more likely than others. It looks identical and it is wrong.

For any sampling answer, say the probability out loud. If you cannot state why each outcome is equally likely, it probably is not.
